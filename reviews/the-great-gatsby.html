F. Scott Fitzgerald

The Great Gatsby

Fiction Classic

There is a dock, and there is a green light across the water. Gatsby stretches his hand toward it, trembling, reaching for something that has already slipped away. I know that gesture. I have lived it.

When I stand at the dock, I too look far away and try to touch the past, with a heart of longing. The light is always there — steady, distant, indifferent to how much you ache for it. You can see it. You can almost feel it. But it does not come closer.

"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."

Fitzgerald wrote a short novel, but he buried an entire ocean of feeling inside it. Gatsby is not really about wealth or parties or Long Island summers. It is about the unbearable weight of wanting something back — a moment, a version of someone, a version of yourself that existed only in the presence of another.

The parties are loud so the silence doesn't win. The shirts are beautiful so the ugliness of loss stays hidden. Everything Gatsby builds is an altar to a memory. And the tragedy is not that he fails — it is that he never stops believing he won't.

I finished this book in a single sitting. Then I sat with it for days. Some books you read. This one reads you.
