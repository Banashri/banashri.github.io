Week 3 — Idioms and Expressions

What I Learned

This week I dug into idioms — those strange little phrases that mean nothing like what the words suggest. "Break a leg" means good luck. "Bite the bullet" means to endure something painful. "The ball is in your court" means it's your turn to act. What fascinates me is how naturally native speakers use these without ever thinking about how bizarre they sound. I started keeping a small notebook of every idiom I encounter in conversation or reading.

What Surprised Me

Many English idioms have direct parallels in other languages, but with completely different images. In Hindi we say "nau do gyarah hona" for running away — nine, two, eleven — which makes no literal sense either. It made me realise that every language has its own private poetry hidden inside everyday speech. The idioms that surprised me most were the ones rooted in history: "turn a blind eye" comes from Admiral Nelson, who literally put a telescope to his blind eye to avoid seeing a signal to retreat.

What I Struggled With

Using idioms at the right moment is harder than understanding them. I know what "a piece of cake" means, but in conversation I either forget to use it or it comes out forced, like I'm performing rather than speaking. There's a timing to it — a naturalness — that I haven't developed yet. I also mixed up "on the fence" with "over the hill" once, which changed my meaning entirely.

A Sentence I'm Proud Of

"I was on the fence about the new project, but once I saw the prototype, it was a no-brainer."

Two idioms in one sentence, and it felt natural when I said it.
